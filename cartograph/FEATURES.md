## Architecture in one paragraph

Cartograph + Stage produce a **slab** — a baked, flattened, fortified, secure, optimized artifact under `public/baked/<look>/` — that the LS app builds on top of like a building on a foundation. Authoring is slow, careful, fortification work; the slab is fast, dumb, impenetrable substrate the app trusts unconditionally. **Preview** is the slab inspection environment, used during authoring to stress-test the slab (GPU profiler, phone-aspect frame, per-layer cost matrix) before handing it off. The LS app and its end-user features (place cards, businesses, accounts) live downstream.

**The architecture is the deliverable. Lafayette Square is the v1 instance.** Other neighborhoods will pour their own slabs from the same toolkit; other operators will do the pouring. Every design decision is in service of that kit ambition.

**Say what the neighborhood is, then pour it in one click — "the next town pours itself" made real.** The **Extent** tool is the front door. Type the name of a place; if the world's map already knows its boundary, you start with it. If it doesn't — or the boundary a bureaucrat drew isn't the one people actually mean — you click the streets that bound it, in any order, on a labeled aerial photo, and watch the ring close. A street that doesn't meet the others lights amber and says so, so you're never left guessing which one is wrong. What you get is a boundary made of **real streets meeting at real junctions**, which is why it never slices a building or cuts a block in half. Then you correct the margin the way anyone would — loop the commercial strip that isn't really part of the hood, wave a single landmark in because it gives the place its character. One button — **Bake** — runs the whole build and drops you into the finished, rendered neighborhood. No terminal, no scripts. **Śródmieście, Łódź was poured this way in an afternoon: a boundary the city itself publishes, 2,954 buildings, in a country the tool had never seen.** *(As-built: `ARCHITECTURE.md §The Extent tool & the Pour` / `INTAKE.md §0.5`; operator flow: `OPERATIONS.md §Extent`.)*

## The conceptual model

Cartograph is recursive — each authoring step makes a truth-claim the next builds on:

- **centerlines** (Survey) → *provable truth*: streets exist here, with this geometry
- **+ thickness** (Section) → *provable truth*: this street is N meters wide on each side
- **+ dimension** (ribbons) → *emergent*: the 3D cross-section per street
- **+ finish** (Stage) → *authored*: materials, palettes, lighting, sky

**Designer is fortification, not invention.** The operator splits, defines, classifies, marks caps and couplers — against aerial-photo ground truth — but doesn't author geometry. All "other" data (buildings, parcels, landmarks, land use) flows through Designer for the same fortification treatment.

**The inputs are authoritative, not guessed.** That "provable truth" rests on real sources — each town's own assessor **parcels** (declared per town, found by searching its official GIS), **operator-measured** street widths, OpenStreetMap geometry, **building footprints** (a city's own where published — NYC's with roof heights and permanent ids — else ML-derived), **street trees** (a city's own live inventory where published, each tree's species and size) — each fortified against max-resolution aerials. It is grounded in the *actual* municipal and survey record, not one generic feed. *(Full provenance: `INTAKE.md`.)*

**The pedestrian cross-section is authored, and the default is already correct.** The operator adjusts **sidewalks and tree lawns** per block edge — swap which side the walk sits on, tune the depths, or flip a cul-de-sac's whole cap — and can **return any edge, or the whole scene, to the default at any time.** The default is not blank: the kit reads each street's measured width to decide whether that frontage really has a tree lawn, then lays the strips out at **ADA-compliant depths** — and a road the map data calls an **expressway** gets its curb but no sidewalk or tree lawn at all. So the map arrives correct and the operator *corrects*, rather than drawing a city from scratch. **And every corner gets a curb ramp**, so the pavement always reaches the street. What it looks like falls out of the two block edges meeting there, and the operator authors none of it: where the walk already runs along the kerb on both sides, there is nothing to do and nothing appears; where it is set back on one side, the grass slopes out along that block so the pavement arrives flush; and where it is set back on both, the grass runs its full width right up to the corner and stops, and the pavement steps out across it to meet the street. Three looks, one rule — the pavement always gets to the kerb. **Where a street meets the corner, it also gets its curb cuts** — the drop and its warning strip, placed where the town's own records say the kerb drops, else to the state's or town's standard (one diagonal cut, or a pair facing the crossings), or by the operator corner by corner; a bend in one street gets none, and a town with no standard yet says so on every pour rather than guessing. **Crosswalks join each ramp to the matching ramp across the street**, painted to the same standard (two lines, or zebra bars). *(Operator manual: `OPERATIONS.md §Section`; as-built: `SECTION.md §3.1` + `§4` "THE RAMP".)*

**The hard geometry is handled by the kit, automatically.** Turning-circle cul-de-sacs (tangent "keyhole" curbs), divided carriageways with medians (straight outer fronts; the median is a *derived* walked face), complex intersections — each corner with its **curb cuts** in the town's style (diagonal, perpendicular or none, overridable per leg) and the **crosswalks** running square across the street from those cuts, painted to match the corner and reaching even the far kerb of a T where the town's standard says so — the neighborhood edge (streets that fade out instead of being chopped), and **the shoreline of a waterfront town** all resolve **from the data** — not hand-drawn. **A road is drawn because of what kind of road it is, not because someone named it**: an unnamed expressway or a nameless residential lane is a street like any other. Resolving them from the data is what lets the kit pour the *next* town without anyone redrawing it. Where the data genuinely can't reach, the operator overrides directly; automation keeps widening what it resolves on its own, guarded by an automatic correctness suite. *(As-built: `RIBBONS.md` / `SKELETON.md`; the bug-class scoreboard: `POLYGON-FIRST §5`.)*

**The ground has the shape the ground actually has.** The kit finds the town's own laser survey — the same national lidar a surveyor would order — and pours the real relief: the rise of a street, the bank behind a seawall, the roll of a dune. Nobody types a height and nobody downloads a gigabyte; the kit reads only the few megabytes of the survey it needs. **Where the survey does not reach, the kit says so** rather than quietly flattening the town: a place with no lidar is marked as searched-and-absent, and a flat pour becomes a decision someone made instead of a silence nobody noticed. ⭐ And it takes **one** survey rather than the best pieces of several, because butting a 2015 flight against a 2021 one seams the ground exactly where it genuinely moved — which on a barrier beach is most of what you came to see. ⭐ **And the hills are lit by their own slope** — the lighting reads the same relief the ground is drawn with, at the survey's own spacing. *(As-built: `fetch-dem.mjs`; the heightfield is `bake-terrain.js`; the lighting is `terrainShader.js` `TERRAIN_NORMAL`, guarded by `checks/claims-ground-normals-come-from-the-terrain.mjs`.)*

**A town on the water pours like any other town.** On a lakefront or riverfront neighborhood, roughly a third of the map is water. This one reads the shoreline out of the same map data as the streets and treats it as what it is: **a real edge of the land.** The water is flat and meets the land wherever the land dips below it; beneath it a sand bed slopes away from the shore the way a real beach does, so the shallows show their bottom and the water deepens into its own colour — and from the street or from the water there is never a gap between the two. The blocks and yards between the last street and the waterline are ordinary land with an ordinary land use, and the streets that run down to the water are drawn as streets right to their ends. **A shoreline is also easier than a street, not harder**, because it is absolute: a kerb is authored and negotiable, a coastline is simply where the land ends. **A lake and an open sea are read the same way even though the map records them differently** — a lake is drawn as a closed body, while an ocean coast is only ever a set of loose shore fragments with the land on one side, so the kit stitches those fragments back into one shore and takes the water from it. Huron, Ohio, on Lake Erie was the first waterfront town off the line; Provincetown, on the Atlantic, is the first on open water. *(As-built: `RIBBONS.md §1`; the shoreline module is `coastline.mjs`.)*

**Highways are built to code, not drawn by eye.** A freeway, its ramps and the grass between its carriageways come from the road's own lanes and the state's published design standards; every width is traceable to its source, and anything no permitted source answers is marked unknown on the pour rather than guessed.

**The kit reads the land, not just the lawn.** A neighborhood's ground is whatever that place actually is — a cornfield, a beach, a brownfield, a marsh, a rail corridor, a cemetery — and a map that knows only the words its first town used will quietly render all of it as somebody's front yard. That is worse than leaving it blank, because it looks right. This kit reads the land-use vocabulary out of the same map data as the streets and paints each kind as itself; and where a town brings a word the kit has never seen, the pour **says so by name** rather than guessing. ⭐ The point is not the list of words — any list is the wrong list for the next town — it is that **nothing can be silently dropped**: every land-describing tag a town carries either becomes a kind of ground, or is declared, in writing, as something we looked at and decided was not ground. There is no third category, and a check enforces it on towns nobody has opened. ⭐ **The land is painted by its evidence, polygon by polygon.** A block can be a row of houses on one side and a field on the other; each part is painted as itself, from the map's own land-use shapes and then each assessor parcel, and ground no data describes is shown as undescribed, never given an invented class. ⭐ **Some ground is green but not free**: a field is planted with a crop, an orchard with fruit trees in rows — the kit distinguishes *"a tree may grow here"* from *"this is already growing something"*, which are different facts that a two-way switch cannot hold. ⭐ **Crop rows grow where crops actually are** — on mapped fields, and where the map is silent, wherever USDA's national crop survey saw crops that year; a meadow, a farmyard, a roadside strip or a farmhouse yard is grass. ⭐ **A field keeps the calendar.** Farmland runs a season — bare dirt, tilled raised rows, sprouts, full green rows, then harvest plays it back to dirt — on dates that belong to the town (read from USDA's published planting and harvest windows for its state, not typed in), each field starting on its own day within that window and running its rows along its own long axis. A town whose state has no published dates shows its fields as bare dirt and says so. ⭐ **And all of it is painted as one clean sheet.** A map of a hilly town used to come apart along exactly the lines that matter — the edge between a field and a wood, a park and the street beside it, torn and lacy where the two should simply meet. The cause was the map being built in layers a few millimetres apart, which is fine on a flat table and **arithmetically impossible on ground that curves**: two layers described at different levels of detail drift apart by up to a metre and a half as the hill bends, and which one is on top flips wherever the bend does. So the kit stopped stacking. The painted ground is now **resolved at bake time into a single surface** — each layer cut to what is actually visible, one plane, edges still real geometry and vector-sharp at street level. ⚠️ It was invisible on a small flat neighborhood and severe on a large contoured one, which is exactly the kind of defect a kit cast around one town cannot see. *(As-built: `derive.js` `OSM_TO_LU` + `OSM_LU_DECLARED`, `lu-policy.mjs`, `bake-ground.js`'s paint-stack flatten; the guards are `checks/claims-every-lu-tag-has-a-home.mjs` + `checks/claims-coplanar-groups-do-not-overlap.mjs`.)*

**Stage is the second authoring environment** — the *theatrical* sense of "stage": where the look gets staged (materials, palettes, lighting, sky, post-FX), finalized into the slab on Bake. The look panel is organized by what you're *doing* — tone & colour · light & shadow · the sky & air · the glowing sources — not by which render pass a knob happens to hit *(reorganized by intent 2026-06-30; `scratch/LOOK-PANEL-TAXONOMY.md`)*.

**The light lives in the ground, and the whole look is a curve across the day.** The lamps' pools of light, the soft contact shadows under every tree and lamp, and the way a trunk's lowest metre takes on the color of the ground beneath it are all *baked into the ground itself* — contour-correct, never floating decals. The surfaces themselves are kept **low-chroma and lit by the sky's own colour** — a hemisphere skylight that washes them with the dawn's cool violet or golden-hour amber — so the neighborhood takes its mood from the *light*, not from paint. And every look channel — sky, bloom, exposure, lamp brightness, the Gateway Arch's foot-uplights — is authored as a **time-of-day curve**, so one Look renders golden-hour, high-noon, and deep-night faithfully on the neighborhood's own clock. ⭐ **And every town is lit, whether or not anyone ever mapped its lamps.** Where the map records real street lamps, they stand where they stand; where it records none — most small towns — the kit lines the streets itself, on the tree lawn, at the spacing real towns' own lamps are measured to keep. Each town chooses its lamp post — the kit's plain modern post by default, or a period lamp like Lafayette Square's Victorian torchiere. Every lamp knows whether it is real or invented, so an operator can see which is which and correct either — and its light falls on the trees and the house fronts around it as it does on the ground beneath — a wall facing a lamp brightens, fading with distance and with the angle — all in the lamp's own colour, and none of it by day. Neighbouring pools overlap along a street the way real ones do, because each town's pool reach is set from its own lamp spacing — and the operator tunes the bulb, the glow around it, the pools' strength and their radius, and the trees, each on its own. ⭐ **And a lamp lights whatever ground it stands over** — asphalt, curb, sidewalk, lawn, tree lawn, sand, field, gravel path — in one way, so a pool never breaks at the edge of the grass; a check builds every surface's real material and fails if one draws without it. *(As-built: `STAGE.md`; the baked ground-contact maps: `SLAB-CONTRACT §3.1/§3.2`.)*

**The sun casts real shadows, on a town of any size.** Every building throws a shadow that lands where the building is, softens the way a real shadow softens, and is there whether the neighborhood is eight hundred metres across or four kilometres. That sounds like table stakes and it was not: the shadow pass drew the buildings *without* the foundations that seat them on the hillside, so it shadowed a town buried in its own terrain — and the patch of ground it covered at all was a box sized, once, to the first neighborhood we ever built. On a larger town, three quarters of the map could not receive a shadow at any setting. **Both are properties of the town now, read from the town.** The softness is authored in **metres on the ground** — a number that means the same thing in every neighborhood, instead of one that quietly meant something different in each. And when the kit cannot work out how big a town is, **it stops casting and says so**, rather than guessing a size and drawing a confident, wrong picture. The same discipline holds the **ambient occlusion** — the soft darkening where surfaces meet, which is most of what makes a map read as lit rather than coloured-in: it used to be possible for a re-bake to quietly throw it away, leaving a town flat-lit with nothing anywhere saying so, and now a stale one is **refused by name**. *(As-built: `ARCHITECTURE.md §8`; the guards are `checks/claims-displaced-casters-have-a-depth-material.mjs` + `checks/claims-the-ao-belongs-to-its-ground.mjs`.)*

**Everything sits exactly on the ground — one surface, nothing floats.** Trees, lamps and gravel paths anchor to the *single rendered ground surface* (a baked per-object anchor), as the buildings' foundations do, rather than guessing their height off a smooth field the coarse ground only approximates; every tree image is framed from the ground up. ⭐ Each anchor file records **which ground it was measured on**, so a re-surveyed terrain cannot leave trees floating on the old one — the map refuses stale anchors by name and says how to re-bake them. *(As-built: `ARCHITECTURE.md §8` "Ground conformance"; guarded by `checks/claims-anchors-know-their-terrain.mjs` + `checks/claims-a-tree-card-starts-at-the-ground.mjs`.)*

**One look, every surface, every device — by construction.** The post-FX pipeline (depth-of-field, bloom, ambient occlusion, film grade, grain) is *declared once* and installed identically wherever the map renders — the live site, the authoring Stage, and the Preview inspector — with no forked render paths that quietly drift apart. Author a Look once and it looks the same on all three; mobile is a *dial-down of the same pipeline*, not a separate one. *(As-built: `ARCHITECTURE.md §8` "Render pipeline"; `PREVIEW.md`.)*

**The Bake is the slab pour** — the publish moment for cartograph. The artifact ships to the LS app, not directly to end users. The operator watches it happen: every step listed as it runs, a real progress bar where a step knows its total, a Cancel that names what it stopped, and a plain message naming the step if one fails. A Bake that would re-pour a town because the kit's own code changed asks first.

**The slab carries the operator's *full* authored product.** This is load-bearing. Anything authored in cartograph — geometry AND optics (sky, atmosphere, post-FX, exposure, time-of-day, per-shot camera, materials, neon, lamp glow) — must travel through the bake into the slab. The deployed runtime trusts the slab unconditionally and cannot reach back into the authoring store, so **anything authored-but-not-baked is invisible** — the product silently degrades to "operator geometry + procedural-default optics," which isn't the product. The product is what the operator sees in Stage. *(Gap inventory: `BACKLOG.md` "Slab completeness.")*

**The camera flies clean through buildings.** Move the camera inside a building and it dissolves out of the way — a clear, uninterrupted shot through the neighborhood instead of a jarring hollow cross-section. Roofs you pass over stay solid. It's automatic, with no setting to hunt for.

**Aesthetics + performance are co-equal, non-negotiable.** Aesthetics are the differentiator (what separates this from generic 3D maps); performance is equally important and invisible (mobile playback can't compromise). The whole authoring-time complexity exists to guarantee both at runtime.

*(The geometry doctrine — tiles, ribbons, corners, curbs — lives in `RIBBONS.md`; read it before any geometry work.)*

## The operator environments

*(What each is, for the pitch. The knobs — every slider and gesture — live in `OPERATIONS.md`.)* **Extent** is the front door — the intake tool described up top ("say what the neighborhood is, then pour it"); the three below are where the map gets made.

### 1. Designer (`/cartograph.html`, `inDesigner` mode)
**Owns: fortification of all spatial + tabular data against aerial-photo ground truth.** Top-down orthographic, compass-N up, paired with max-resolution georeferenced aerials (never shipped). The operator traces and corrects the street network, paths, lots, park boundary; classifies land use; integrates buildings, parcels, landmarks, lamps — splits, defines, marks caps, sets couplers, but doesn't author geometry. **Toolbar = views, Panel = tools:** the toolbar carries view controls (Aerial, Look picker, Stage); the panel's 3-part pill (Survey · Section · Design) selects the authoring tool. A new scene routes through the *same* pipeline, never a scene-only branch (where to validate: `docs/agents/AGENT-VALIDATION-SURFACES.md`). Output: `data/raw/{centerlines,measurements}.json` + `data/clean/overlay.json`.

**The Marker — operator↔kit triage.** A freehand pen layer over the map, independent of the authoring tools. The operator circles anything that needs attention — a faceted loop, a mis-traced lot, a feature to revisit — and the strokes persist (`data/clean/marker_strokes.json`) as a durable, shareable punch-list drawn *in place* on the geometry, so "the two loops that are still faceted" is a thing you point at, not a thing you describe. A companion server endpoint (`/analyze`) reports the parcels and blocks under the strokes, turning a circle into a query — *what's under here?* It's how a human aims the kit at the next bug without leaving the map. *(The gestures — eraser · undo · clear → `OPERATIONS.md`.)*

### 2. Stage (`/cartograph.html`, shot modes)
**Owns: look authoring — the theatrical sense.** Perspective camera, multiple "shots" each with their own framing, atmosphere, time-of-day. The operator takes Designer's fortified data as truth and dresses it in a chosen aesthetic ("Look"). Concretely, **you can change the colors** (per-layer + per-building palettes — and **each neighbourhood's neon is its own**: its category colours, which colour its signs and its chips in the Ward — and **so is its identity**: the emoji it goes by (which is also what its places are rated in, and is drawn in the Ward's header as a white silhouette, engraved, or in colour), what it calls its locals (Lafayette Square's *Townies*; any town's own word, *Locals* until it chooses), its accent and the tint of lit roofs, chosen in one **Identity** panel that Sources lists as a to-do until the town has chosen — or suggested from the mark itself, with every colour held to the Ward's contrast rules), **set materials** (roughness, metalness, texture, emissive), **author the sky** (gradient, sun/moon, ambient, mist, halo), **shape the image with post-FX** — a cinematic **depth-of-field** that pulls *real* focus (the near sharp, the distance melting — which doubles as an invisible cover for far detail to taper) with a gentle softening reserved for the hero, and a **bloom** that lets a bright sky *backlight* the hero and makes the lamps and neon truly glow, plus exposure, color grade, film grain, ambient occlusion, tune the **streetlamps and the Gateway Arch's uplights**, and compose an **authored Hero camera move** on a **timeline** — fly the camera to a view and key it at the moment you want, ⌥-drag keys to change the pace (a plain drag only scrubs, so looking never moves a key), and choose a **bounce** or a seamless **loop** (a lighthouse turn), previewed exactly as it ships, on any town. **And a visitor enters that path at a different point each time they arrive**, so the neighborhood does not open on the same frame forever — a random moment in the move the operator authored, so nothing outside those keyframes is ever shown — and because **every one of those is a time-of-day curve**, a single Look renders golden-hour, noon, and deep-night faithfully. **The plan view opens where the operator framed it** — one button sets the town's Browse frame, and a phone, the Ward's map half and a desktop all open on that same square of ground — while Stage's own camera stays wherever the operator is working. The artist spends most of their time here. Output: `public/looks/<id>/design.json` per Look. *(Every knob, by card, and how to drive it: [`OPERATIONS.md`](OPERATIONS.md) "Stage.")*

**A Look is a whole authored day.** Not one frozen moment — the operator paints the neighborhood across **eight moments of the real sun** (dawn → sunrise → noon → golden → sunset → dusk → night → deep night), and nearly every atmospheric and post channel rides that clock: sky brightness, sun/moon/ambient/hemisphere light, the lamps, mist, halo, exposure, bloom, **depth-of-field**, warmth, shadow fill. So one Look can carry a real artistic arc — a moody dawn, a blown-out noon, an electric-colour sunset, a blue-moonglow dusk, a dark night — and each slot is its own composed picture. **DoF** is the romance *and* the invisible level-of-detail cover (near sharp, distance melting away). **Bloom** lets a bright sky backlight the hero and makes lamps and neon truly glow. The sky's colour follows the real sun's altitude over the town, so every season and latitude gets its own dawn. The streetlamps are **photocell-realistic** — lit before dawn, dark after sunrise. And the whole authored day **bakes into the slab**, so the public app sees the operator's exact look at every slot. ⭐ **Every town is born with a designed day** — a lavender dawn, a bright tilt-shift noon, a dazzling golden hour, a blue hour where the gaslamps and neon come on, a stylish night of deep blacks and cold glittering lamps, and a deep night where the town goes quiet and the Milky Way and the constellations take the sky. It is keyed to the sun's own moments, so it follows the real sun in any town and season; the operator overrides any of it, and what they leave alone keeps following the kit.


### 3. Preview (`/preview.html`)
**Owns: the publish-confidence gate — *know* the slab will ship before it does, no push-and-wait.** Renders exactly what production renders, plus the inspection toolkit (per-layer cost, phone frame, layer-toggle matrix, TOD scrub) — and the proving ground where the operator (a) reads each channel's *tax against a target-device budget* — benchmarked to **two real reference phones** (iPhone 16 Pro Max as the ceiling, a Samsung Galaxy A54/A55 as the floor we hold ourselves to), not the operator's desktop, (b) makes the **per-surface deployment call** (which effects ship to desktop and to phones; it autosaves, and the next Publish ships it) right beside the gauge that responds, and (c) catches the three things a fast desktop hides — thermal, memory, and crash-on-transition. If a layer is too hot here, it surfaces before a mobile user feels it; if something looks right in Stage but wrong in Preview, the bake didn't propagate. **And it ships, with buttons that say what they ship** (*Publish slab · player · slab + player*; a Promote that cannot go prints why): *Publish* is the upload, and gives each town a private preview link, and *Promote to Production* puts that town — alone — on its own domain (e.g. `provincetown.online`), running the exact bytes that were previewed, in whichever player its staging site plays (the Ward, or the old player until its cutover); no other town changes. Model: `PREVIEW.md`; shipping: `PUBLISH.md §0.5`. *(Every control and how to read it: [`OPERATIONS.md`](OPERATIONS.md) "Preview.")*

## What every town knows, wherever it's viewed from

- **Every building knows its street address**, taken from the town's own records, its 911 address points or OpenStreetMap, matched by where it actually sits and never guessed. A building whose address is unknown or disputed says so.
- **Every town keeps its own clock.** Open hours, the neon and the sky's time of day follow the town's time zone, so a visitor in another zone sees exactly what a neighbour sees.
- **An open place's neon reads from anywhere, in its own colour.** Up close it is a real glass tube, a few centimetres thick; from overhead or across town it hands off to a glowing line of fixed on-screen width, so a sign never balloons into a blown-out ring near the camera and never vanishes on the horizon. Every sign shows its category's colour, never a white core with the colour only in the glow. **And neon belongs to the place, not the building:** each open place lights its own stretch of wall — the face its street address marks, or its building's front on its own street — so a building with three shops shows three signs, and one closed shop never darkens an open neighbour.
- **Neighbouring trees meet as volumes, not cut-outs.** A far tree is a photograph on a card; two cards side by side used to slice through each other in straight lines that swept across the crowns as the camera moved. Each card now carries the depth of every leaf it shows, so overlapping crowns interleave the way real ones do.
- **The trees move with the town's real wind, and you can see a gust.** One wind field crosses the whole town, from its own weather, and every tree reads it, so a gust is one visible front moving through the canopy. On top of that wind every tree has its own endemic rustle, so the canopy is alive even in still air — and a passing gust is guaranteed to show on screen at every distance instead of vanishing below a pixel.
- **The map frames what you ask for, all of it:** choose a category and every one of its places fits in view.
